# Economic Indicators

## Overview

Economic indicators are statistics that provide information about the condition and direction of an economy.

Investors use economic indicators to understand factors that may affect:

* Economic growth.
* Corporate earnings.
* Consumer spending.
* Interest rates.
* Employment.
* Inflation.
* Investment conditions.
* Financial markets.

Important economic indicators include:

* Gross Domestic Product (GDP).
* Inflation.
* Interest rates.
* Unemployment.
* Consumer spending.
* Retail sales.
* Industrial production.
* Consumer confidence.
* Business confidence.

Economic indicators can provide useful context, but they do not predict stock prices with certainty.

---

# Gross Domestic Product

**Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** measures the total monetary value of final goods and services produced within an economy over a particular period.

GDP is commonly used as a broad measure of economic activity.

When GDP grows, economic activity is generally increasing.

When GDP contracts, economic activity is generally weakening.

---

# GDP Growth

GDP growth measures how economic output changes over time.

For example:

**Previous GDP = $20 trillion**

**Current GDP = $20.4 trillion**

The economy has grown by approximately:

**2%**

Positive GDP growth can indicate expanding economic activity.

However, GDP growth does not automatically mean every company will perform well.

---

# Real vs Nominal GDP

There is an important distinction between real and nominal GDP.

### Nominal GDP

Nominal GDP measures economic output using current prices.

### Real GDP

Real GDP adjusts for changes in prices, making it more useful for measuring changes in actual economic output.

Investors and economists often focus on real GDP growth when evaluating economic expansion.

---

# GDP and Stock Markets

Economic growth can affect companies through:

* Consumer demand.
* Business investment.
* Corporate revenue.
* Employment.
* Profitability.

Strong economic growth can support corporate earnings.

However, strong GDP growth can also contribute to higher inflation or interest rates, which may create challenges for some companies.

Therefore:

**Strong GDP growth ≠ guaranteed stock-market gains**

---

# Inflation

Inflation refers to a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services.

When inflation rises, the purchasing power of money decreases.

For example:

If prices increase by 5%, the same amount of money generally buys fewer goods and services than before.

---

# Consumer Price Index

The **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** measures changes in the prices paid by consumers for a basket of goods and services.

CPI is one commonly used measure of inflation.

It can provide information about changes in the cost of living.

---

# Core Inflation

Core inflation generally excludes food and energy prices from the calculation.

The reason is that food and energy prices can be particularly volatile.

Removing them can provide another view of underlying inflation trends.

However, consumers still experience changes in food and energy prices, so headline inflation remains important.

---

# Inflation and Stocks

Inflation can affect companies in several ways.

Higher input costs can increase expenses for businesses.

For example:

* Raw materials become more expensive.
* Wages may increase.
* Transportation costs may increase.
* Energy costs may increase.

Companies may respond by:

* Raising prices.
* Accepting lower profit margins.
* Reducing costs.

The effect depends on the company's ability to pass higher costs to customers.

---

# Inflation and Consumers

Higher inflation can reduce consumers' purchasing power.

Consumers may respond by:

* Reducing discretionary spending.
* Delaying large purchases.
* Switching to cheaper products.

This can affect companies differently depending on their products and customers.

---

# Interest Rates

Interest rates represent the cost of borrowing money or the return earned on certain forms of lending and saving.

Central banks influence short-term interest rates through monetary policy.

Interest rates can affect:

* Consumer borrowing.
* Business investment.
* Mortgage costs.
* Savings.
* Bond yields.
* Stock valuations.

---

# Central Banks

Central banks are institutions responsible for monetary policy and other functions within an economy.

Examples include:

* Federal Reserve in the United States.
* European Central Bank.
* Bank of England.
* Bank of Japan.
* Monetary Authority of Singapore.

Central banks may adjust monetary policy in response to economic conditions.

---

# Monetary Policy

Monetary policy refers to actions taken by a central bank to influence economic and financial conditions.

A central bank may attempt to:

* Control inflation.
* Support employment.
* Stabilise economic conditions.

The exact objectives differ between central banks.

---

# Raising Interest Rates

When a central bank raises interest rates, borrowing generally becomes more expensive.

Potential effects include:

* Lower consumer borrowing.
* Reduced business investment.
* Higher financing costs.
* Greater attractiveness of some interest-bearing assets.
* Lower present values for future cash flows.

Higher rates can therefore create pressure on some stock valuations.

---

# Lowering Interest Rates

When interest rates fall, borrowing generally becomes cheaper.

Potential effects include:

* Increased borrowing.
* Increased business investment.
* Greater consumer spending.
* Lower financing costs.
* Higher present values for future cash flows.

However, rate cuts can also occur because economic conditions are weakening.

Therefore:

**Interest-rate cuts do not automatically mean stocks will rise.**

---

# Discount Rates and Valuation

Interest rates can affect stock valuation through discount rates.

Future cash flows are discounted to calculate their present value.

A higher discount rate generally results in a lower present value.

A lower discount rate generally results in a higher present value.

This relationship is particularly important for companies whose expected cash flows are far into the future.

---

# Unemployment

The unemployment rate measures the percentage of the labour force that is unemployed and actively seeking employment.

A rising unemployment rate can indicate weakening labour-market conditions.

A falling unemployment rate can indicate stronger employment conditions.

---

# Employment and Consumer Spending

Employment affects household income.

Higher employment can support:

* Consumer spending.
* Housing demand.
* Retail activity.
* Services spending.

Weak employment can reduce consumer spending and economic activity.

Because consumer spending represents a significant portion of economic activity in many economies, employment data can be important for investors.

---

# Retail Sales

Retail sales measure consumer purchases of retail goods.

They can provide information about consumer demand.

Strong retail sales may indicate healthy consumer spending.

Weak retail sales may indicate that consumers are becoming more cautious.

However, retail sales can be affected by:

* Seasonal factors.
* Inflation.
* Changes in consumer preferences.
* Weather.
* Temporary events.

---

# Consumer Confidence

Consumer confidence measures how optimistic or pessimistic consumers feel about the economy and their financial situation.

Higher confidence may support spending.

Lower confidence may encourage consumers to save more and reduce discretionary spending.

Confidence does not always translate directly into actual spending behaviour.

---

# Business Confidence

Business confidence measures how optimistic businesses are about economic conditions and future activity.

High business confidence may support:

* Hiring.
* Capital investment.
* Expansion.
* Production.

Low confidence may lead businesses to delay investments or hiring.

---

# Manufacturing Activity

Manufacturing indicators provide information about industrial production and business activity.

Examples include:

* Industrial production.
* Manufacturing output.
* Purchasing Managers' Index (PMI).

A rising manufacturing PMI can indicate expanding activity.

A declining PMI can indicate weakening activity.

---

# Purchasing Managers' Index

The **Purchasing Managers' Index (PMI)** is a survey-based indicator of business conditions.

A PMI reading above 50 is commonly associated with expansion.

A reading below 50 is commonly associated with contraction.

The interpretation depends on the specific PMI and methodology.

---

# Consumer Spending

Consumer spending is an important component of economic activity.

Consumers spend money on:

* Housing.
* Food.
* Transport.
* Entertainment.
* Healthcare.
* Retail goods.
* Services.

Companies that depend heavily on consumer spending can be particularly sensitive to changes in economic conditions.

---

# Recession

A recession is a period of significant decline in economic activity.

During a recession, businesses may experience:

* Lower demand.
* Lower revenue.
* Reduced hiring.
* Lower investment.
* Reduced profitability.

However, the exact definition and identification of a recession can vary by country and institution.

---

# Recession and Stock Markets

Stock markets may react to expectations about recessions before official economic data confirms one.

Investors may anticipate:

* Lower earnings.
* Higher unemployment.
* Lower consumer spending.
* Monetary-policy changes.

Therefore, stock prices can decline before economic conditions visibly deteriorate.

Similarly, markets can begin recovering before the economy fully recovers.

---

# Economic Cycles

Economies often move through different stages.

A simplified economic cycle can include:

1. Expansion.
2. Peak.
3. Contraction.
4. Trough.
5. Recovery.

Different industries can perform differently during different stages.

---

# Expansion

During an expansion:

* Economic output increases.
* Employment may increase.
* Consumer spending may increase.
* Business investment may increase.

Cyclical companies may benefit from stronger demand.

---

# Peak

A peak represents a point where economic activity reaches a high before slowing.

At this stage:

* Growth may begin slowing.
* Inflation may remain elevated.
* Interest rates may be relatively high.

Markets may begin anticipating a change in economic conditions.

---

# Contraction

During contraction:

* Economic activity weakens.
* Businesses may reduce investment.
* Employment growth may slow.
* Consumer spending may weaken.

Some defensive sectors may become relatively attractive to investors.

---

# Trough and Recovery

A trough represents a low point in economic activity.

During recovery:

* Economic activity begins improving.
* Employment may increase.
* Consumer demand may recover.
* Business investment may increase.

Stock markets may begin recovering before economic indicators fully improve.

---

# Leading Indicators

Leading indicators attempt to provide information about future economic activity.

Examples include:

* Stock-market movements.
* Building permits.
* New orders.
* Consumer expectations.
* Certain financial conditions.

Leading indicators can be useful but are not perfect predictions.

---

# Coincident Indicators

Coincident indicators generally move alongside current economic activity.

Examples include:

* Employment.
* Personal income.
* Industrial production.

They can help describe the current state of the economy.

---

# Lagging Indicators

Lagging indicators tend to change after economic conditions have already changed.

Examples may include:

* Certain unemployment measures.
* Corporate profits.
* Labour costs.

They can help confirm economic trends after they have developed.

---

# Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy refers to government decisions involving:

* Government spending.
* Taxation.
* Government borrowing.

Governments may use fiscal policy to influence economic activity.

For example, increased government spending may support demand during a weak economy.

---

# Government Spending and Stocks

Government spending can affect companies through:

* Infrastructure projects.
* Defence spending.
* Healthcare programmes.
* Public services.
* Subsidies.

The impact varies depending on which industries and companies benefit from the spending.

---

# Government Debt

Government borrowing can increase when governments spend more than they collect in revenue.

High government debt can create concerns about:

* Future taxation.
* Interest payments.
* Fiscal sustainability.

However, the significance of government debt depends on factors such as:

* Economic growth.
* Interest rates.
* Currency.
* Government institutions.
* Debt maturity.
* Investor confidence.

---

# Exchange Rates

Exchange rates describe the value of one currency relative to another.

For example:

**USD/SGD = 1.35**

means approximately 1 US dollar is worth 1.35 Singapore dollars at that exchange rate.

Exchange rates can affect multinational companies.

A company earning revenue in foreign currencies may see its reported results change when those currencies are converted.

---

# Commodity Prices

Commodity prices can affect inflation and corporate profitability.

Examples include:

* Oil.
* Natural gas.
* Copper.
* Gold.
* Agricultural commodities.

Higher commodity prices can benefit commodity producers while increasing costs for companies that consume those commodities.

---

# Economic Indicators and Different Industries

Economic conditions do not affect all companies equally.

For example:

### Banks

Can be affected by:

* Interest rates.
* Credit conditions.
* Loan demand.
* Defaults.

### Consumer Discretionary

Can be affected by:

* Consumer confidence.
* Employment.
* Disposable income.

### Technology

Can be affected by:

* Interest rates.
* Business investment.
* Growth expectations.

### Energy

Can be affected by:

* Oil prices.
* Natural-gas prices.
* Global demand.

### Utilities

Can be affected by:

* Interest rates.
* Regulation.
* Energy prices.

# Why Macroeconomic Data Could Be Useful

Macroeconomic variables could potentially provide additional context.

For example:

**High inflation + high interest rates**

could represent a different market environment from:

**Low inflation + low interest rates**

A future model could potentially incorporate macroeconomic features.

However, adding more variables does not automatically improve predictive performance.

They would need to be tested carefully.

---

# Avoiding Data Leakage

When incorporating economic indicators into a machine-learning model, the publication date must be considered.

For example:

Suppose an economic report is officially released on:

**10 March**

A model predicting stock returns on:

**5 March**

must not use that report.

Even if the dataset records the economic period as February, the information was not yet available to investors on 5 March.

Using future information would create **data leakage**.

Economic indicators provide macroeconomic context.

> Inflation has increased.

> TSLA predicted 5-day return = +0.86%.

The first describes the broader economic environment.

The second is a model prediction for an individual stock.

One should not automatically be interpreted as causing the other.

# Important Limitations

Economic indicators have several limitations.

## Data Revisions

Economic statistics can sometimes be revised after their initial publication.

## Time Lags

Some indicators are published after the economic activity occurred.

## Measurement Errors

Economic statistics are estimates and may contain uncertainty.

## Different Interpretations

Investors may interpret the same economic data differently.

## Expectations

Markets often react to how data compares with expectations rather than the absolute number alone.

---

# Expectations vs Economic Data

Suppose inflation is reported at:

**3.0%**

If investors expected:

**4.0%**

the news may be interpreted positively.

If investors expected:

**2.0%**

the same 3.0% inflation figure may be interpreted negatively.

Therefore:

**Market reaction depends on both actual data and expectations.**

---

# Economic Indicators and Market Timing

Economic indicators should not be treated as precise market-timing signals.

For example:

* Strong GDP does not guarantee rising stocks.
* High inflation does not guarantee falling stocks.
* Rate cuts do not guarantee rising stocks.
* Rising unemployment does not guarantee falling stocks.

Stock prices depend on many interacting factors.

---

# Best Practices

When interpreting economic indicators:

* Consider multiple indicators together.
* Compare data with expectations.
* Check the publication date.
* Consider revisions.
* Understand the economic cycle.
* Consider industry-specific effects.
* Avoid treating one indicator as a complete market forecast.
* Distinguish economic information from stock-specific predictions.
* Avoid using information that was unavailable at the prediction time.

---

# Common Misconceptions

### Strong GDP always means stocks will rise.

False.

Stock prices depend on expectations, valuations, interest rates, earnings, sentiment, and other factors.

---

### Higher inflation always causes stocks to fall.

False.

The effect depends on the economy, company, sector, interest rates, and investor expectations.

---

### Interest-rate cuts always cause stocks to rise.

False.

Rate cuts can sometimes occur because the economy is weakening.

---

### A recession means every stock falls.

False.

Different industries and companies can perform differently during recessions.

---

### Economic indicators directly predict stock prices.

False.

Economic indicators provide information about economic conditions but do not guarantee future stock-market movements.
