# Fundamental Analysis

## Overview

Fundamental analysis is an approach to evaluating an investment by examining the underlying financial and economic characteristics of a company.

Rather than focusing primarily on historical price movements, fundamental analysis considers factors such as:

* Revenue.
* Earnings.
* Profitability.
* Cash flow.
* Debt.
* Assets and liabilities.
* Valuation.
* Competitive position.
* Industry conditions.
* Future growth opportunities.

The goal is to understand the financial condition and economic characteristics of a company and assess whether its current market valuation appears reasonable relative to those characteristics.

Fundamental analysis does not guarantee that a stock will increase in price.

---

# Fundamental Analysis vs Technical Analysis

The two approaches focus on different types of information.

| Fundamental Analysis | Technical Analysis |
| -------------------- | ------------------ |
| Company financials   | Market prices      |
| Revenue              | Returns            |
| Earnings             | Momentum           |
| Cash flow            | Volume             |
| Debt                 | Volatility         |
| Profitability        | Trends             |
| Valuation            | Price patterns     |
| Business performance | Market behaviour   |

An investor can use both approaches.

For example, an investor could use fundamental analysis to determine whether a company appears financially strong and technical analysis to examine recent market behaviour.

---

# Financial Statements

Companies generally provide several important financial statements.

The three major statements are:

1. Income statement.
2. Balance sheet.
3. Cash flow statement.

These statements provide different information about the company.

---

# Income Statement

The income statement reports a company's financial performance over a specific period.

Important items include:

* Revenue.
* Operating expenses.
* Operating income.
* Interest expenses.
* Taxes.
* Net income.

---

# Revenue

Revenue represents the income generated from a company's business activities before expenses are deducted.

For example, if a company sells $100 million worth of products during a year, its revenue may be $100 million.

Revenue growth can provide information about whether a company's business is expanding or contracting.

However, increasing revenue does not necessarily mean increasing profits.

A company can increase revenue while simultaneously experiencing rising costs.

---

# Revenue Growth

Revenue growth measures how revenue changes over time.

A simplified calculation is:

**Revenue Growth = (Current Revenue - Previous Revenue) ÷ Previous Revenue**

For example:

Previous revenue = $100 million

Current revenue = $120 million

Revenue growth:

**($120m - $100m) ÷ $100m = 20%**

Revenue growth should be considered alongside profitability and cash flow.

---

# Net Income

Net income is the profit remaining after expenses, interest, and taxes have been accounted for.

A company can have:

* Positive revenue.
* Positive operating income.
* Positive net income.

However, the amount and quality of earnings should be considered together with cash flow and other financial information.

---

# Earnings Per Share

Earnings per share, commonly abbreviated as **EPS**, measures a company's earnings relative to the number of shares outstanding.

A simplified calculation is:

**EPS = Net Income ÷ Shares Outstanding**

For example, if a company earns $10 million and has 2 million shares:

**EPS = $5**

EPS is commonly used in valuation metrics such as the P/E ratio.

---

# Profit Margin

Profit margin measures how much of a company's revenue remains as profit.

A simplified net profit margin is:

**Net Profit Margin = Net Income ÷ Revenue × 100**

For example:

Revenue = $100 million

Net income = $10 million

Net profit margin = **10%**

Profit margins can help investors compare profitability over time or between companies.

---

# Operating Margin

Operating margin measures the percentage of revenue remaining after operating expenses.

A simplified calculation is:

**Operating Margin = Operating Income ÷ Revenue × 100**

Operating margin can provide information about the profitability of a company's core operations.

---

# Balance Sheet

The balance sheet provides information about a company's financial position at a specific point in time.

It includes:

* Assets.
* Liabilities.
* Shareholders' equity.

The fundamental accounting relationship is:

**Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity**

---

# Assets

Assets are resources controlled by a company.

Examples include:

* Cash.
* Investments.
* Inventory.
* Property.
* Equipment.
* Accounts receivable.

---

# Liabilities

Liabilities represent obligations owed by the company.

Examples include:

* Loans.
* Bonds.
* Accounts payable.
* Lease obligations.

High levels of debt can increase financial risk, particularly when interest rates are high or business conditions deteriorate.

---

# Shareholders' Equity

Shareholders' equity represents the residual interest in a company's assets after liabilities are deducted.

A simplified relationship is:

**Equity = Assets - Liabilities**

---

# Debt

Debt allows companies to finance operations and expansion.

Debt is not automatically bad.

A company may use borrowing to:

* Build facilities.
* Purchase equipment.
* Fund expansion.
* Acquire another company.

However, excessive debt can increase financial risk.

Investors should consider debt relative to:

* Earnings.
* Cash flow.
* Assets.
* Interest expenses.
* Industry conditions.

---

# Debt-to-Equity Ratio

The debt-to-equity ratio compares a company's debt with shareholders' equity.

A simplified calculation is:

**Debt-to-Equity = Total Debt ÷ Shareholders' Equity**

A higher ratio generally indicates greater financial leverage.

However, acceptable debt levels differ significantly between industries.

---

# Cash Flow

Cash flow measures the movement of cash into and out of a business.

The cash flow statement generally separates cash flows into:

* Operating activities.
* Investing activities.
* Financing activities.

Cash flow is important because accounting profits do not always represent the amount of cash available to the company.

---

# Operating Cash Flow

Operating cash flow measures cash generated by the company's core business operations.

Consistently positive operating cash flow can be an important indicator of business strength.

However, it should be considered alongside other financial measures.

---

# Free Cash Flow

Free cash flow generally represents cash generated by the business after necessary capital expenditures.

A simplified representation is:

**Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow - Capital Expenditures**

Companies may use free cash flow to:

* Reinvest in the business.
* Pay dividends.
* Repurchase shares.
* Reduce debt.
* Hold cash.

---

# Valuation

Valuation attempts to determine how much an investment may be worth relative to its financial characteristics.

A company can be financially strong while its stock is expensive.

Likewise, a company can have weak financial performance while appearing cheap.

Therefore:

**Good company ≠ automatically good investment**

The price paid for an investment matters.

---

# Price-to-Earnings Ratio

The **P/E ratio** compares a company's share price with its earnings per share.

A simplified calculation is:

**P/E = Share Price ÷ EPS**

For example:

Share price = $100

EPS = $5

P/E = **20**

This means investors are paying $20 for every $1 of annual earnings represented by the ratio.

---

# Interpreting P/E

A high P/E ratio can indicate that investors expect relatively strong future growth.

A low P/E ratio may indicate that a company is cheaper relative to its earnings.

However, a low P/E does not automatically mean a stock is undervalued.

Similarly, a high P/E does not automatically mean a stock is overvalued.

P/E should be compared with:

* Historical valuations.
* Industry peers.
* Expected growth.
* Profitability.
* Interest rates.
* Business quality.

---

# Price-to-Sales Ratio

The price-to-sales ratio compares a company's market value with its revenue.

A simplified calculation is:

**P/S = Market Capitalization ÷ Revenue**

This ratio can sometimes be useful for companies with low or negative earnings where a P/E ratio may not be meaningful.

---

# Price-to-Book Ratio

The price-to-book ratio compares the company's market value with its book value.

A simplified calculation is:

**P/B = Market Capitalization ÷ Book Value**

It is particularly relevant in industries where tangible assets are important.

---

# Return on Equity

Return on equity, or **ROE**, measures how effectively a company generates profit relative to shareholders' equity.

A simplified calculation is:

**ROE = Net Income ÷ Shareholders' Equity × 100**

A higher ROE can indicate stronger profitability relative to the company's equity base.

However, high debt can also increase ROE, so the ratio should not be interpreted alone.

---

# Return on Assets

Return on assets, or **ROA**, measures profitability relative to the company's assets.

A simplified calculation is:

**ROA = Net Income ÷ Total Assets × 100**

This can help investors understand how effectively a company uses its assets to generate earnings.

---

# Earnings Growth

Earnings growth measures how a company's profits change over time.

Consistent earnings growth can indicate that the company's business is expanding.

However, investors should examine whether earnings growth is supported by:

* Revenue growth.
* Cash flow.
* Sustainable margins.
* Reasonable debt levels.

---

# Competitive Advantage

Fundamental analysis can also involve qualitative factors.

These may include:

* Brand strength.
* Intellectual property.
* Network effects.
* Switching costs.
* Economies of scale.
* Customer loyalty.
* Competitive positioning.

These characteristics can influence a company's ability to maintain profitability over time.

---

# Industry Analysis

A company's performance should also be considered in the context of its industry.

Factors can include:

* Competition.
* Regulation.
* Consumer demand.
* Economic cycles.
* Technological changes.
* Supply chains.
* Interest rates.

A strong company operating in a declining industry may face different challenges from a strong company operating in a growing industry.

---

# Fundamental Analysis Example

Suppose two companies have the following characteristics:

| Metric         | Company A | Company B |
| -------------- | --------: | --------: |
| Revenue Growth |       15% |        5% |
| Net Margin     |       20% |        8% |
| P/E            |        30 |        12 |
| Debt           |       Low |      High |
| Cash Flow      |    Strong |      Weak |

Company A appears stronger based on several operational measures.

However, Company A also has a higher valuation.

Company B may appear cheaper but carries greater financial risk.

This illustrates why fundamental analysis requires examining multiple factors rather than relying on one metric.

# Common Misconceptions

### A profitable company is always a good investment.

False.

The company's stock price may already reflect high expectations.

---

### A low P/E means a stock is undervalued.

Not necessarily.

A low P/E may reflect weak growth expectations or higher business risk.

---

### High revenue growth guarantees strong investment returns.

False.

A company can grow revenue rapidly while remaining unprofitable or generating weak cash flow.

---

### Debt is always bad.

False.

Debt can help companies finance productive investments.

The important consideration is whether the debt level is sustainable.

---

### Financial statements tell you exactly what a stock will do next.

False.

Financial statements describe historical and current financial conditions. They do not guarantee future stock-price movements.

# Combining Fundamental and Technical Analysis

Investors can use both approaches.

For example:

### Step 1 — Fundamental Analysis

Assess:

* Financial health.
* Revenue growth.
* Profitability.
* Debt.
* Cash flow.
* Valuation.

### Step 2 — Technical Analysis

Assess:

* Momentum.
* Trend.
* Volatility.
* Recent returns.
* Trading activity.

### Step 3 — Risk Assessment

Consider:

* Diversification.
* Investment horizon.
* Position size.
* Market conditions.

This provides a broader assessment than relying on a single metric.

---

# Best Practices

When performing fundamental analysis:

* Read multiple financial statements.
* Compare companies with relevant industry peers.
* Examine revenue and earnings trends.
* Consider cash flow.
* Assess debt levels.
* Evaluate valuation.
* Consider the company's competitive position.
* Avoid relying on one financial ratio.
* Consider whether current expectations are already reflected in the share price.

# Frequently Asked Questions

### What is fundamental analysis?

Fundamental analysis evaluates a company using financial, economic, and business information.

---

### What are the most important financial statements?

The three primary financial statements are:

1. Income statement.
2. Balance sheet.
3. Cash flow statement.

---

### What is P/E?

P/E, or price-to-earnings ratio, compares a company's share price with its earnings per share.

---

### What is EPS?

EPS stands for earnings per share and represents earnings relative to the number of shares outstanding.

---

### Is a high P/E bad?

Not necessarily.

A high P/E may reflect expectations of strong future growth.

---

### Is fundamental analysis better than technical analysis?

Neither approach is universally better.

They answer different questions and can be used together.

---

### Does the Financial Advisor Bot perform fundamental analysis?

The current model primarily uses technical and market-derived features.

It should therefore not be presented as a complete fundamental-analysis system.
