# Stock Valuation

## Overview

Stock valuation is the process of estimating the value of a company's shares.

Investors use valuation methods to assess whether a stock's current market price appears:

* Relatively low.
* Reasonable.
* Relatively high.

Valuation is not an exact science.

Different assumptions about growth, profitability, interest rates, and risk can produce substantially different estimates of value.

Therefore, an estimated fair value should not be treated as a guaranteed future stock price.

---

# Market Price vs Estimated Value

A stock has a market price determined by buying and selling in the market.

An investor may also estimate what the stock is worth based on financial or economic assumptions.

These are different concepts.

**Market Price**

The current price at which the stock trades.

**Estimated Value**

An estimate of what the stock may be worth based on a particular valuation method and assumptions.

For example:

Current price = $100

Estimated value = $120

An investor might describe the stock as potentially undervalued relative to that valuation estimate.

However, the estimate could be wrong.

---

# Intrinsic Value

Intrinsic value refers to an estimate of what an asset is worth based on its underlying economic characteristics.

For a company, intrinsic value may be estimated using:

* Future cash flows.
* Earnings.
* Assets.
* Growth expectations.
* Risk.
* Discount rates.

There is no single universally accepted intrinsic value for a company.

Different valuation models can produce different results.

---

# Fair Value

Fair value is commonly used to describe an estimated reasonable value for an investment.

For example:

Current market price = $80

Estimated fair value = $100

An investor may consider the stock potentially undervalued based on that estimate.

However, fair value depends on the assumptions and methodology used.

---

# Undervalued vs Overvalued

A stock may be described as **undervalued** when its market price appears lower than an estimated value.

A stock may be described as **overvalued** when its market price appears higher than an estimated value.

For example:

**Estimated value = $150**

**Market price = $120**

The stock could be considered potentially undervalued by $30 under that valuation model.

However, this does not mean the stock will necessarily rise to $150.

---

# Discounted Cash Flow

One of the most widely known valuation methods is the **Discounted Cash Flow**, or **DCF**, model.

DCF estimates the present value of future cash flows.

The basic idea is:

**Money received in the future is worth less today than the same amount of money received today.**

DCF therefore:

1. Estimates future cash flows.
2. Estimates a discount rate.
3. Discounts those cash flows back to their present value.
4. Estimates a terminal value.
5. Combines the values.

---

# Present Value

The present value of a future cash flow can be represented as:

**PV = CF ÷ (1 + r)^t**

Where:

* **PV** = Present Value.
* **CF** = Future Cash Flow.
* **r** = Discount Rate.
* **t** = Number of periods.

For example, if an investor expects $110 one year from now and uses a 10% discount rate:

**PV = $110 ÷ 1.10 = $100**

The $110 future cash flow therefore has a present value of $100 under the 10% discount rate assumption.

---

# DCF Formula

A simplified DCF model can be represented as:

**Value = Σ [FCFₜ ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ] + Terminal Value**

Where:

* **FCFₜ** = Free cash flow in period t.
* **r** = Discount rate.
* **t** = Time period.

The model estimates the value of future cash flows in today's terms.

---

# Forecast Period

A DCF model usually begins with an explicit forecast period.

For example:

* Year 1.
* Year 2.
* Year 3.
* Year 4.
* Year 5.

The investor estimates free cash flow for each year.

Longer forecast periods introduce greater uncertainty because it becomes harder to predict future business conditions.

---

# Terminal Value

Companies are generally expected to continue operating beyond the explicit forecast period.

A DCF model therefore needs a way to estimate the value of cash flows beyond the forecast period.

This is called **terminal value**.

One common method is the perpetual-growth approach.

A simplified formula is:

**Terminal Value = FCFₙ₊₁ ÷ (r - g)**

Where:

* **FCFₙ₊₁** = Free cash flow after the forecast period.
* **r** = Discount rate.
* **g** = Long-term growth rate.

---

# Terminal Growth Rate

The terminal growth rate represents the assumed long-term growth rate after the explicit forecast period.

For example:

**Terminal growth rate = 2.5%**

A reasonable terminal growth assumption should generally be consistent with long-term economic and business conditions.

A company cannot realistically grow at extremely high rates forever.

---

# Discount Rate

The discount rate reflects the required return or risk associated with future cash flows.

A higher discount rate generally produces a lower present value.

A lower discount rate generally produces a higher present value.

For example:

If future cash flows remain unchanged:

**Higher discount rate → Lower estimated value**

**Lower discount rate → Higher estimated value**

---

# Why Interest Rates Matter

Interest rates can affect valuation.

When interest rates increase, discount rates may also increase.

Higher discount rates reduce the present value of future cash flows.

This can particularly affect companies whose valuations depend heavily on cash flows expected far in the future.

However, the relationship between interest rates and stock prices is more complicated than a single formula because interest rates can also affect:

* Economic growth.
* Corporate earnings.
* Borrowing costs.
* Consumer spending.
* Investor behaviour.

---

# Free Cash Flow in Valuation

Free cash flow is often used in DCF models because it represents cash that remains after necessary capital expenditures.

A simplified calculation is:

**Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow - Capital Expenditures**

A company with growing and sustainable free cash flow may have stronger valuation characteristics than a company with declining or highly uncertain cash generation.

However, free cash flow can fluctuate substantially between periods.

---

# Growth Assumptions

Growth assumptions are one of the most important inputs in valuation.

For example, an investor might assume:

**Revenue growth = 10%**

and:

**Long-term growth = 3%**

If actual growth is substantially lower, the estimated valuation may fall.

If actual growth is substantially higher, the estimated valuation may increase.

---

# Valuation Sensitivity

Valuation models can be highly sensitive to assumptions.

Suppose a DCF model estimates:

**Fair value = $100**

A small change in the discount rate or long-term growth assumption might change the estimate substantially.

For example:

| Scenario     | Estimated Value |
| ------------ | --------------: |
| Conservative |             $75 |
| Base Case    |            $100 |
| Optimistic   |            $135 |

This demonstrates why valuation should often be considered as a range rather than a precise number.

---

# Scenario Analysis

Scenario analysis evaluates multiple possible outcomes.

Common scenarios include:

### Bear Case

Assumes:

* Lower growth.
* Lower margins.
* Higher risk.
* Higher discount rate.

### Base Case

Uses assumptions considered reasonably plausible.

### Bull Case

Assumes:

* Higher growth.
* Stronger margins.
* Lower risk.
* Stronger business performance.

Scenario analysis can help investors understand how sensitive a valuation is to assumptions.

---

# Margin of Safety

The concept of a **margin of safety** involves purchasing an investment at a price meaningfully below an estimated value.

For example:

Estimated value = $100

Market price = $70

Difference = $30

The investor has a potential margin of safety because the purchase price is below the estimated value.

However, a margin of safety does not eliminate investment risk.

The estimated value itself may be incorrect.

---

# Valuation Multiples

Investors often use valuation multiples to compare companies.

Common examples include:

* P/E.
* P/S.
* P/B.
* EV/EBITDA.
* Price-to-Free-Cash-Flow.

These ratios compare market value with different financial measures.

---

# Comparable Company Analysis

Comparable company analysis involves comparing a company with similar companies.

For example:

| Company   | P/E |
| --------- | --: |
| Company A |  20 |
| Company B |  25 |
| Company C |  22 |
| Company D |  21 |

If Company A has a P/E of 20 while similar companies trade around 22–25, an investor may investigate whether Company A is relatively inexpensive.

However, differences in:

* Growth.
* Profitability.
* Debt.
* Business quality.
* Risk.

may justify different valuations.

---

# Historical Valuation

Investors can compare a company's current valuation with its historical valuation.

For example:

| Period             | P/E |
| ------------------ | --: |
| Historical Average |  25 |
| Current            |  18 |

A lower current P/E may indicate that the stock is cheaper relative to its historical valuation.

However, the company's business conditions may also have changed.

A lower valuation may be justified if future growth expectations have declined.

---

# Growth vs Valuation

Growth and valuation should generally be considered together.

Consider two companies:

|                 | Company A | Company B |
| --------------- | --------: | --------: |
| P/E             |        15 |        35 |
| Earnings Growth |        5% |       25% |

Company B has a much higher valuation but also significantly higher growth.

It would be inappropriate to conclude that Company A is automatically better simply because its P/E is lower.

---

# PEG Ratio

The PEG ratio attempts to relate P/E to earnings growth.

A simplified formula is:

**PEG = P/E ÷ Expected Earnings Growth Rate**

For example:

P/E = 30

Expected earnings growth = 15%

PEG = **2**

PEG can provide another perspective on valuation.

However, it depends heavily on future growth estimates, which may be inaccurate.

---

# Enterprise Value

Enterprise value, or **EV**, attempts to represent the value of a company's operating business while considering debt and cash.

A simplified representation is:

**EV = Market Capitalization + Debt - Cash**

The exact calculation can include additional adjustments.

Enterprise value is commonly used with metrics such as EBITDA.

---

# EV/EBITDA

EV/EBITDA compares enterprise value with earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortisation.

It is commonly used to compare companies with different levels of debt.

However, EBITDA is not the same as free cash flow.

Investors should therefore avoid treating EV/EBITDA as a complete measure of company value.

---

# Asset-Based Valuation

Some companies can be valued partly by examining their assets and liabilities.

Examples may include:

* Property companies.
* Financial institutions.
* Resource companies.

An asset-based approach may be less useful for businesses whose value comes primarily from intangible assets such as:

* Software.
* Brands.
* Intellectual property.
* Networks.

---

# Dividend Discount Model

The **Dividend Discount Model**, or DDM, estimates the value of a stock based on the present value of future dividends.

A simplified constant-growth version is:

**Value = D₁ ÷ (r - g)**

Where:

* **D₁** = Expected dividend next year.
* **r** = Required return.
* **g** = Long-term dividend growth rate.

This model is more naturally suited to companies with relatively stable and predictable dividend policies.

---

# Valuation Is Uncertain

A valuation estimate is not a fact.

It depends on assumptions about the future.

Important uncertainties include:

* Revenue growth.
* Profit margins.
* Interest rates.
* Competition.
* Regulation.
* Economic growth.
* Capital expenditure.
* Consumer demand.
* Management decisions.

Because these factors are uncertain, valuation should generally be treated as an estimate.

---

# Overvaluation Does Not Mean Immediate Decline

A stock can appear expensive relative to a valuation model and continue increasing in price.

For example:

Estimated value = $100

Current price = $150

The stock may appear overvalued under the model.

However, if future growth turns out to be much stronger than expected, the estimated value may increase.

Therefore:

**Overvalued ≠ guaranteed to fall**

---

# Undervaluation Does Not Mean Immediate Increase

Similarly, a stock may appear undervalued and continue declining.

Possible reasons include:

* Earnings deterioration.
* New negative information.
* Industry problems.
* Incorrect valuation assumptions.
* Higher interest rates.
* Increased business risk.

Therefore:

**Undervalued ≠ guaranteed to rise**

---

# Stock Valuation vs Market Price

Market prices incorporate the actions and expectations of many investors.

A stock can trade at a price that differs significantly from an individual's estimated value.

This does not necessarily mean the market is wrong.

It may mean that investors have different assumptions about:

* Growth.
* Risk.
* Future earnings.
* Interest rates.
* Competitive conditions.

---

# Valuation and Market Sentiment

Valuation and sentiment can sometimes move in opposite directions.

For example:

**Strong sentiment + high valuation**

may occur when investors are highly optimistic about future growth.

Alternatively:

**Negative sentiment + low valuation**

may occur when investors have become pessimistic.

A low valuation does not automatically make a company attractive if its underlying business is deteriorating.

* `Rolling_MaxDD`
* `Relative_Return`

* P/E-based fair value.

Therefore, a chatbot BUY signal should **not** be described as proof that a stock is fundamentally undervalued.

---

# Predicted Return vs Valuation

These concepts are different.

### Predicted Return

The model estimates the potential return over its prediction horizon.

For example:

**Predicted 5-Day Return = +0.86%**

This is a model prediction.

### Estimated Intrinsic Value

A valuation model might estimate:

**Estimated value = $350**

This is an estimate based on assumptions about the company's future financial performance.

The two measurements should not be confused.

---

# Example: Tesla

Suppose Tesla has:

**Current price = $300**

A DCF model estimates:

**Fair value = $260**

The stock could be described as potentially expensive relative to that DCF estimate.

However, the Financial Advisor Bot might simultaneously produce:

**Predicted 5-Day Return = +0.86%**

**Market Rank = 96.9%**

**Signal = BUY**

There is no contradiction.

The two systems are answering different questions.

The valuation model is asking:

> "What might this company be worth based on its future cash flows?"

The ML model is asking:

> "Which stocks currently have stronger predicted short-term returns?"

# Important Distinction

The chatbot should distinguish between:

**Educational information**

and:

**Model-generated predictions**

For example:

> "A DCF model estimates value by discounting future cash flows."

is educational information.

Whereas:

> "The model predicts TSLA will return 0.86% over the next five days."

is a model output.

The two should not be presented as equivalent.

# Best Practices

When analysing stock valuation:

* Understand the valuation method.
* Examine the assumptions.
* Compare multiple valuation methods where appropriate.
* Consider industry differences.
* Examine historical valuations.
* Consider growth expectations.
* Consider financial risk.
* Use scenario analysis.
* Avoid treating fair value as a precise number.
* Consider a range of possible outcomes.
* Remember that market prices can differ from valuation estimates for extended periods.

# Common Misconceptions

### A stock below its estimated fair value will immediately rise.

False.

Valuation estimates can be wrong and prices can remain below estimated value for long periods.

---

### A stock above fair value must fall.

False.

Future growth can exceed expectations, causing valuation estimates to increase.

---

### DCF produces the exact value of a company.

False.

DCF produces an estimate based on assumptions.

---

### A lower P/E always means a better investment.

False.

A low P/E can reflect weaker growth expectations or higher risk.

---

### The Financial Advisor Bot's BUY signal means the stock is undervalued.

Not necessarily.

The current model primarily predicts short-term relative returns using market-derived features. It does not directly calculate intrinsic value.

---

### A positive predicted return means the stock is fairly valued.

False.

Predicted return and valuation are different concepts.

# Frequently Asked Questions

---

---

### What is DCF?

---
