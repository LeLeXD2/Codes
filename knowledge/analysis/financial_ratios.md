# Financial Ratios

## Overview

Financial ratios are measurements calculated from a company's financial information. They help investors evaluate areas such as:

* Valuation.
* Profitability.
* Financial strength.
* Efficiency.
* Debt.
* Growth.

Ratios are useful because they allow investors to compare companies of different sizes and examine how a company's financial characteristics change over time.

However, a financial ratio should rarely be used by itself to make an investment decision.

A ratio should generally be considered alongside:

* Industry averages.
* Historical company performance.
* Business growth.
* Financial statements.
* Economic conditions.
* Other financial ratios.
* The company's overall business situation.

---

# Price-to-Earnings Ratio

The **price-to-earnings ratio**, commonly called **P/E**, compares a company's share price with its earnings per share.

**P/E = Share Price ÷ Earnings Per Share**

For example:

Share price = $100

EPS = $5

P/E = **20**

This means investors are paying $20 for every $1 of annual earnings represented by the ratio.

---

# Interpreting P/E

A relatively high P/E may indicate that investors expect:

* Higher future growth.
* Stronger profitability.
* Lower perceived risk.
* Greater future earnings.

A relatively low P/E may indicate:

* Lower growth expectations.
* Greater perceived risk.
* Weak recent performance.
* Potential undervaluation.

However, a low P/E does **not** automatically mean a stock is cheap.

Similarly, a high P/E does **not** automatically mean a stock is expensive.

P/E should be compared with relevant companies and historical valuations.

---

# Forward P/E

Forward P/E uses expected future earnings rather than historical earnings.

A simplified concept is:

**Forward P/E = Current Share Price ÷ Expected Future EPS**

Because future earnings are estimates, forward P/E depends on the assumptions used to produce those earnings forecasts.

If expected earnings change, the forward P/E can change even when the stock price remains unchanged.

---

# Price-to-Sales Ratio

The **price-to-sales ratio**, or **P/S**, compares a company's market value with its revenue.

**P/S = Market Capitalization ÷ Revenue**

P/S can be useful for companies that have low or negative earnings where P/E may not provide meaningful information.

However, revenue does not tell us how profitable a company is.

Two companies can have identical revenue but very different levels of profit.

---

# Price-to-Book Ratio

The **price-to-book ratio**, or **P/B**, compares market value with book value.

**P/B = Market Capitalization ÷ Book Value**

Book value generally represents the accounting value of shareholders' equity.

P/B can be particularly useful when analysing companies whose assets are an important component of their business.

However, accounting book value may not fully represent the economic value of intangible assets such as:

* Brands.
* Intellectual property.
* Software.
* Customer relationships.

---

# Earnings Per Share

**EPS**, or earnings per share, represents a company's earnings relative to its shares outstanding.

A simplified calculation is:

**EPS = Net Income ÷ Shares Outstanding**

For example:

Net income = $50 million

Shares outstanding = 10 million

EPS = **$5**

EPS is commonly used when calculating the P/E ratio.

---

# EPS Growth

EPS growth measures how earnings per share change over time.

For example:

Previous EPS = $4

Current EPS = $5

EPS growth:

**($5 - $4) ÷ $4 = 25%**

Strong EPS growth can indicate improving profitability.

However, investors should determine whether the growth is sustainable.

---

# Return on Equity

**ROE**, or return on equity, measures how effectively a company generates profit relative to shareholders' equity.

**ROE = Net Income ÷ Shareholders' Equity × 100**

For example:

Net income = $20 million

Shareholders' equity = $100 million

ROE = **20%**

A higher ROE can indicate that a company is generating more profit relative to its equity base.

However, high debt can also increase ROE.

Therefore, ROE should be considered alongside leverage.

---

# Return on Assets

**ROA**, or return on assets, measures profitability relative to total assets.

**ROA = Net Income ÷ Total Assets × 100**

ROA can provide information about how efficiently a company uses its assets to generate earnings.

Companies with different business models may naturally have different ROA levels.

Therefore, ROA is most useful when comparing companies with reasonably similar business models.

---

# Profit Margin

Profit margin measures how much revenue remains as profit.

A simplified net profit margin is:

**Net Profit Margin = Net Income ÷ Revenue × 100**

For example:

Revenue = $200 million

Net income = $20 million

Net profit margin = **10%**

A higher margin generally means that a larger proportion of revenue becomes profit.

---

# Operating Margin

Operating margin focuses on profit from normal business operations.

**Operating Margin = Operating Income ÷ Revenue × 100**

Operating margin can help investors evaluate the profitability of a company's core operations.

Changes in operating margin can provide information about:

* Pricing power.
* Cost control.
* Operating efficiency.
* Competitive pressure.

---

# Gross Margin

Gross margin measures the percentage of revenue remaining after the cost of goods sold has been deducted.

**Gross Margin = (Revenue - Cost of Goods Sold) ÷ Revenue × 100**

For example:

Revenue = $100 million

Cost of goods sold = $60 million

Gross margin = **40%**

Gross margin can help investors understand the economics of a company's products or services.

---

# Debt-to-Equity Ratio

The **debt-to-equity ratio** compares debt with shareholders' equity.

**Debt-to-Equity = Total Debt ÷ Shareholders' Equity**

For example:

Total debt = $50 million

Equity = $100 million

Debt-to-equity = **0.5**

A higher ratio generally indicates greater financial leverage.

However, appropriate debt levels vary substantially between industries.

---

# Why Debt Matters

Debt can help a company finance:

* Expansion.
* Equipment.
* Acquisitions.
* Research and development.
* Working capital.

However, debt also creates obligations.

A company may need to make:

* Interest payments.
* Principal repayments.

High debt can become more problematic when:

* Interest rates increase.
* Revenue declines.
* Cash flow weakens.
* Economic conditions deteriorate.

---

# Current Ratio

The **current ratio** measures a company's current assets relative to current liabilities.

**Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities**

For example:

Current assets = $200 million

Current liabilities = $100 million

Current ratio = **2.0**

A higher current ratio can indicate greater short-term liquidity.

However, an extremely high ratio is not automatically better.

The quality of the company's current assets also matters.

---

# Quick Ratio

The quick ratio is a more conservative liquidity measure.

It generally excludes inventory from current assets.

A simplified calculation is:

**Quick Ratio = (Current Assets - Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities**

This can help assess whether a company could cover short-term obligations using more liquid assets.

---

# Free Cash Flow

Free cash flow is an important measure of a company's ability to generate cash after capital expenditures.

A simplified calculation is:

**Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow - Capital Expenditures**

Positive free cash flow can provide a company with flexibility to:

* Reinvest.
* Pay dividends.
* Repurchase shares.
* Reduce debt.
* Build cash reserves.

---

# Price-to-Free-Cash-Flow

The price-to-free-cash-flow ratio compares a company's market value with its free cash flow.

A lower ratio can indicate that investors are paying less for each unit of free cash flow.

However, interpretation depends on:

* Industry.
* Growth expectations.
* Capital requirements.
* Business quality.
* Future cash-flow expectations.

---

# Dividend Yield

Dividend yield measures annual dividends relative to the share price.

**Dividend Yield = Annual Dividend Per Share ÷ Share Price × 100**

For example:

Annual dividend = $2

Share price = $50

Dividend yield = **4%**

A high dividend yield does not automatically mean a stock is attractive.

The dividend may be high because the company's share price has fallen.

---

# Dividend Payout Ratio

The payout ratio measures how much of a company's earnings are distributed as dividends.

A simplified calculation is:

**Payout Ratio = Dividends ÷ Net Income × 100**

A very high payout ratio may indicate that the company has limited room to maintain dividends if earnings decline.

However, appropriate payout ratios differ between companies and industries.

---

# PEG Ratio

The **price/earnings-to-growth ratio**, or PEG, attempts to consider earnings growth alongside the P/E ratio.

A simplified version is:

**PEG = P/E ÷ Expected Earnings Growth Rate**

For example:

P/E = 30

Expected earnings growth = 15%

PEG = **2**

PEG has limitations because expected growth is uncertain.

Different analysts may have different forecasts.

---

# Enterprise Value to EBITDA

**EV/EBITDA** compares enterprise value with earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortisation.

It is commonly used to compare companies with different capital structures.

The ratio can be useful when analysing companies with significant differences in debt.

However, EBITDA is not the same as cash flow and should not be treated as such.

---

# Comparing Ratios Between Companies

Ratios become more useful when comparisons are made between similar companies.

For example:

| Ratio       | Company A | Company B |
| ----------- | --------: | --------: |
| P/E         |        20 |        35 |
| P/S         |         3 |         6 |
| ROE         |       18% |       22% |
| Debt/Equity |       0.4 |       1.2 |
| Net Margin  |       15% |       18% |

Company B has stronger profitability but also higher valuation and leverage.

It would therefore be inappropriate to conclude that Company A or B is automatically the better investment based on a single ratio.

---

# Ratios and Industry Differences

Financial ratios can differ substantially between industries.

For example:

* Banks have different balance sheets from technology companies.
* Retail companies may operate with different margins from software companies.
* Utilities may have different debt levels from technology companies.

Therefore, comparing a technology company with a bank using only the same ratio may produce misleading conclusions.

Industry-specific comparisons are generally more meaningful.

---

# Ratio Trends

Investors can examine how a company's ratios change over time.

For example:

| Year | Net Margin |
| ---: | ---------: |
| 2022 |         8% |
| 2023 |        10% |
| 2024 |        12% |
| 2025 |        14% |

The increasing margin may indicate improving profitability.

However, investors should investigate why the change occurred.

Possible explanations include:

* Higher prices.
* Lower costs.
* Improved efficiency.
* Temporary conditions.
* Accounting changes.

---

# Using Multiple Ratios

A stronger analysis generally combines multiple measures.

For example:

### Valuation

* P/E.
* P/S.
* P/B.
* EV/EBITDA.

### Profitability

* ROE.
* ROA.
* Gross margin.
* Operating margin.
* Net margin.

### Financial Strength

* Debt-to-equity.
* Current ratio.
* Quick ratio.

### Cash Generation

* Operating cash flow.
* Free cash flow.

Using multiple ratios provides a broader view of a company's financial characteristics.

---

# Ratios Should Not Be Used Alone

A financial ratio is a measurement, not a complete investment thesis.

For example:

> "Company X has a P/E of 10, therefore it is a BUY."

This conclusion is too simplistic.

A low P/E could exist because:

* Earnings are expected to decline.
* The industry is under pressure.
* The company has significant debt.
* Investors perceive high risk.
* The stock has structural problems.

Similarly:

> "Company Y has a P/E of 50, therefore it is a SELL."

This may also be incorrect.

A high P/E could reflect strong expected growth.

# Financial Ratios and Machine Learning

Financial ratios can potentially be used as additional machine-learning features.

For example, a future model could include:

* P/E.
* P/S.
* P/B.
* ROE.
* Debt-to-equity.
* Revenue growth.
* EPS growth.
* Free cash flow.

The model could then combine fundamental and technical information.

However, adding more features does not automatically improve model performance.

Additional features should be evaluated carefully using appropriate validation methods to avoid:

* Overfitting.
* Data leakage.
* Redundant features.
* Look-ahead bias.

# Common Misconceptions

### A low P/E means a stock is cheap.

Not necessarily.

The company may have weak growth prospects or higher risk.

---

### A high ROE always means a company is excellent.

Not necessarily.

High leverage can increase ROE.

---

### High dividend yield means a better investment.

False.

A high yield can sometimes result from a falling share price or an unsustainable dividend.

---

### A high current ratio is always better.

Not necessarily.

Excessively high liquidity can also indicate that assets are not being used efficiently.

---

### Financial ratios predict stock prices.

Not directly.

Ratios provide information about financial characteristics and valuation, but future stock prices depend on many factors.

---

# Best Practices

When using financial ratios:

* Compare companies within relevant industries.
* Examine historical trends.
* Use several ratios together.
* Consider the company's growth rate.
* Examine debt and cash flow.
* Understand how each ratio is calculated.
* Investigate unusual changes.
* Avoid treating one ratio as a BUY or SELL signal.
* Consider the broader economic environment.

# Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a financial ratio?

A financial ratio is a numerical measurement derived from financial information that helps evaluate a company's financial condition, profitability, valuation, or efficiency.

---

### What is the most important financial ratio?

There is no single most important ratio.

The appropriate ratio depends on what the investor is trying to evaluate.

---

### What does P/E mean?

P/E compares a company's share price with its earnings per share.

---

### What does ROE mean?

ROE measures net income relative to shareholders' equity.

---

### What does debt-to-equity measure?

Debt-to-equity measures a company's debt relative to shareholders' equity.

---

### What does a high P/E mean?

A high P/E can indicate that investors expect stronger future earnings growth, although it can also reflect other factors.

---

### Should I buy a stock because its ratios look good?

Not necessarily.

Financial ratios are one part of investment analysis and should be considered alongside the company's business, valuation, risks, market conditions, and investment objectives.

---

The current model primarily uses technical and market-derived features rather than traditional financial ratios.
