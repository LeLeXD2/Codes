# Market Indices

## Overview

A market index is a measurement designed to represent the performance of a group of investments.

Instead of tracking one individual stock, an index tracks a collection of securities according to predefined rules.

Market indices are commonly used to:

* Measure market performance.
* Compare investment performance.
* Track particular sectors or markets.
* Create index funds and ETFs.
* Provide benchmarks for investors and portfolio managers.

Examples of major U.S. stock market indices include:

* S&P 500.
* Dow Jones Industrial Average.
* Nasdaq Composite.
* Nasdaq-100.

---

# What Is a Market Index?

A market index combines the performance of multiple securities into a single measure.

For example, instead of looking at the performance of hundreds of individual companies separately, an index can provide a summary of how its underlying group of companies is performing.

An index is therefore a **measurement**, rather than necessarily being an investment itself.

Investors can often invest in funds designed to track an index.

---

# The S&P 500

The **S&P 500** is one of the most widely followed U.S. stock market indices.

It is designed to represent approximately 500 leading publicly traded companies in the United States.

The index covers a broad range of industries, including:

* Technology.
* Healthcare.
* Financials.
* Consumer companies.
* Industrials.
* Energy.
* Communication services.
* Utilities.

The S&P 500 is commonly used as a broad indicator of the performance of large U.S. companies.

---

# S&P 500 Weighting

The S&P 500 uses a **float-adjusted market-capitalization weighting methodology**.

This means that companies with larger market capitalizations generally have larger weights in the index.

A simplified concept is:

**Company Weight = Company Market Capitalization ÷ Total Index Market Capitalization**

The actual methodology includes adjustments for shares available to public investors and other index rules.

---

# Example of Market-Capitalization Weighting

Suppose an index contains three companies:

| Company   | Market Capitalization |
| --------- | --------------------: |
| Company A |          $600 billion |
| Company B |          $300 billion |
| Company C |          $100 billion |

Total market capitalization:

**$1 trillion**

Simplified weights:

| Company   | Weight |
| --------- | -----: |
| Company A |    60% |
| Company B |    30% |
| Company C |    10% |

Company A therefore has a larger influence on the index.

This means that an index's performance is not necessarily an equal average of all its companies.

---

# S&P 500 Rebalancing and Reconstitution

The S&P 500 is maintained according to rules determining which companies are included and how they are weighted.

Companies can be added or removed when they no longer meet the relevant requirements or when index changes are made.

Index maintenance helps keep the index representative of the intended market segment.

---

# Dow Jones Industrial Average

The **Dow Jones Industrial Average**, commonly called the **Dow** or **DJIA**, tracks 30 large U.S. companies.

Unlike the S&P 500, the Dow uses a **price-weighted** methodology.

This means that stocks with higher share prices have greater influence on the index.

---

# Price Weighting

In a price-weighted index, the relative influence of a company is related to its share price rather than its total market capitalization.

A simplified representation is:

**Index Weight ∝ Share Price**

For example:

| Company | Share Price |
| ------- | ----------: |
| A       |        $200 |
| B       |        $100 |
| C       |         $50 |

Company A has approximately twice the price influence of Company B and four times the price influence of Company C in a simplified price-weighted calculation.

The actual Dow calculation uses a divisor to account for events such as stock splits.

---

# Why the Dow Uses a Divisor

Stock splits can change a company's share price without changing the company's overall market value.

For example, suppose a stock trading at $100 undergoes a 2-for-1 split.

The share price becomes approximately $50, but shareholders still own the same overall economic value.

A price-weighted index therefore uses a divisor to prevent events such as stock splits from creating artificial changes in the index.

---

# Nasdaq Composite

The **Nasdaq Composite** is an index covering a large number of securities listed on the Nasdaq Stock Market.

It includes companies from many industries but has substantial exposure to technology and growth-oriented companies.

The Nasdaq Composite is therefore often associated with technology stocks, although it is not exclusively a technology index.

---

# Nasdaq-100

The **Nasdaq-100** tracks 100 of the largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq Stock Market according to its index methodology.

It has significant exposure to large technology and growth companies.

The Nasdaq-100 is therefore different from the broader Nasdaq Composite.

---

# Nasdaq Composite vs Nasdaq-100

| Feature                | Nasdaq Composite                   | Nasdaq-100                               |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Coverage               | Broad Nasdaq-listed securities     | 100 large non-financial Nasdaq companies |
| Number of constituents | Many                               | 100                                      |
| Financial companies    | Can be included                    | Excluded under methodology               |
| Technology exposure    | High                               | High                                     |
| Purpose                | Broad Nasdaq market representation | Large non-financial Nasdaq companies     |

# Index Weighting Methods

Different indices can use different weighting methodologies.

Common approaches include:

1. Market-capitalization weighting.
2. Price weighting.
3. Equal weighting.
4. Fundamental weighting.
5. Factor-based weighting.

---

# Market-Capitalization Weighting

Market-capitalization weighting assigns larger weights to companies with larger market values.

A simplified formula is:

**Weight = Company Market Capitalization ÷ Total Market Capitalization**

Advantages include:

* Reflects company size.
* Easy to understand.
* Usually requires relatively little turnover.
* Represents the market value of the underlying companies.

A potential disadvantage is that larger companies can dominate the index.

---

# Equal Weighting

An equal-weighted index gives each constituent approximately the same weight.

For example, an index containing 100 companies could assign:

**1%**

to each company.

This means a small company has the same initial influence as a large company.

Equal-weighted strategies generally require periodic rebalancing because price movements cause the weights to drift.

---

# Price Weighting

Price-weighted indices give greater influence to stocks with higher share prices.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average is a well-known example.

Price weighting can produce substantially different results from market-capitalization weighting.

---

# Fundamental Weighting

Some index methodologies use fundamental company characteristics when determining weights.

Possible factors include:

* Revenue.
* Earnings.
* Cash flow.
* Dividends.
* Book value.

The purpose is to weight companies according to measures of their economic characteristics rather than solely their market capitalization or share price.

---

# Index Performance

When an index rises, it means the index's calculated value has increased according to its methodology.

For example:

Index value at start = 5,000

Index value at end = 5,250

Percentage change:

**(5,250 - 5,000) ÷ 5,000 × 100 = 5%**

This does not mean every company in the index increased by 5%.

Some companies may have increased substantially, some may have declined, and others may have changed very little.

---

# Indexes Are Not Equal Averages

A common misconception is that an index represents the average performance of every company equally.

This is usually incorrect.

The effect of each company depends on the index's weighting methodology.

For example, a market-capitalization-weighted index gives greater influence to larger companies.

Therefore, a large company's movement can have a much larger effect on the index than a smaller constituent.

---

# Benchmarks

A benchmark is a reference point used to evaluate the performance of an investment or portfolio.

For example, an investor holding a portfolio of large U.S. companies may compare its performance against the S&P 500.

Suppose:

Portfolio return = 12%

S&P 500 return = 10%

The portfolio outperformed the benchmark by:

**2 percentage points**

This comparison can provide context for evaluating investment performance.

---

# Benchmark Does Not Mean Guarantee

A benchmark is a comparison tool.

It does not represent a guaranteed return.

For example, the S&P 500 can decline during a particular period.

An investor should therefore avoid interpreting an index's historical performance as a guaranteed future result.

---

# Choosing an Appropriate Benchmark

The benchmark should be relevant to the investment being evaluated.

For example:

* U.S. large-cap portfolio → S&P 500 may be appropriate.
* Technology-focused portfolio → Nasdaq-related benchmark may be relevant.
* International portfolio → An international equity index may be more appropriate.
* Bond portfolio → A relevant bond index may be appropriate.

Comparing every portfolio against the S&P 500 may not provide a meaningful evaluation.

---

# Market Index vs ETF

An index and an ETF are different things.

### Index

An index is a measurement calculated according to a set of rules.

### ETF

An ETF is an investment fund that trades on a stock exchange.

Some ETFs are designed to track particular indices.

For example, an ETF may attempt to replicate the performance of the S&P 500.

The ETF itself is an investable security, while the index is the benchmark or measurement it attempts to follow.

---

# Index Funds

An index fund is a fund designed to track a particular market index.

The fund attempts to reproduce the index's performance, subject to factors such as:

* Fees.
* Trading costs.
* Tracking differences.
* Taxes.
* Fund structure.

Index investing is often associated with passive investing.

---

# Passive Investing

Passive investing generally aims to track a market index rather than actively selecting individual securities in an attempt to outperform the market.

For example, an investor could invest in a fund designed to track the S&P 500.

The investor is therefore gaining exposure to the group of companies represented by the index rather than selecting individual stocks.

---

# Active Investing

Active investing involves making investment decisions with the goal of achieving a particular outcome, potentially including outperforming a benchmark.

An active investor may:

* Select individual stocks.
* Change portfolio allocations.
* Analyse financial statements.
* Analyse market trends.
* Adjust positions based on market conditions.

Active strategies can involve greater research, trading activity, and costs.

---

# Market Index vs Stock Ranking

A market index and the Financial Advisor Bot's stock ranking serve very different purposes.

## Market Index

An index:

* Represents a predefined group of securities.
* Uses a defined weighting methodology.
* Measures market or segment performance.
* Acts as a benchmark.
* Does not normally predict which individual stock will perform best.

## Financial Advisor Bot Ranking

The chatbot:

* Uses a machine-learning model.
* Predicts future returns.
* Ranks stocks relative to one another.
* Produces BUY/HOLD/SELL signals.
* Attempts to identify stocks with stronger or weaker predicted performance.

Therefore:

**Index = measurement of a group**

**Model ranking = prediction and comparison of individual stocks**

---

# Example

Suppose the S&P 500 contains a group of large U.S. companies.

The index may rise by 1% today.

At the same time, the Financial Advisor Bot could rank the stocks:

| Stock   | Predicted 5-Day Return | Rank | Signal |
| ------- | ---------------------: | ---: | ------ |
| Stock A |                   2.0% |  99% | BUY    |
| Stock B |                   1.5% |  96% | BUY    |
| Stock C |                   0.4% |  60% | HOLD   |
| Stock D |                  -0.5% |  20% | HOLD   |
| Stock E |                  -1.5% |   2% | SELL   |

The index tells us how the broader group performed.

The model ranking attempts to identify which individual stocks are expected to perform relatively better or worse.

---

# Market Rank in the Financial Advisor Bot

The chatbot's **Market Rank** is a cross-sectional ranking.

For example:

**Market Rank = 96.9%**

means the stock's predicted return is higher than approximately 96.9% of the stocks in the model's comparison universe.

It does **not** mean:

* The stock has a 96.9% probability of rising.
* The stock will outperform the S&P 500 by 96.9%.
* The stock is guaranteed to produce a positive return.

This distinction is important when explaining chatbot results.

---

# Index Benchmark vs Model Benchmark

The chatbot can potentially use a market index as a benchmark.

For example, a strategy could be evaluated against:

**S&P 500**

The comparison could include:

* Total return.
* CAGR.
* Volatility.
* Sharpe ratio.
* Sortino ratio.
* Maximum drawdown.

This helps determine whether the model-driven strategy adds value compared with simply holding a broad market benchmark.

---

# Buy-and-Hold Benchmark

A simple benchmark for the Financial Advisor Bot is a buy-and-hold strategy.

For example:

**Initial Capital = $10,000**

If the benchmark increases by 10%:

**Final Value = $11,000**

The chatbot's strategy can then be compared against this result.

If the model produces only a 4% return over the same period, the strategy has underperformed the benchmark despite potentially generating individual BUY signals.

---

# Benchmarking the Financial Advisor Bot

A proper backtest should compare the strategy against a relevant benchmark.

Possible benchmarks include:

* S&P 500.
* Equal-weighted portfolio.
* Buy-and-hold strategy.
* Sector benchmark.
* Other appropriate market indices.

Performance metrics can include:

* Total return.
* CAGR.
* Annual volatility.
* Sharpe ratio.
* Sortino ratio.
* Maximum drawdown.
* Win rate.

---

# Why Benchmarking Matters

A strategy should not be judged only by whether it makes money.

For example:

Strategy return = 8%

S&P 500 return = 15%

The strategy made money but still underperformed the benchmark by:

**7 percentage points**

An investor could potentially have achieved a higher return through the benchmark without using the additional complexity of the model.

Therefore, benchmarking is important when evaluating whether a strategy provides useful value.

# Index Limitations

Market indices have limitations.

These include:

* Index composition can change.
* Weighting methodologies differ.
* Large companies can dominate market-cap-weighted indices.
* An index may not represent the entire economy.
* Different indices cover different market segments.
* Historical index performance does not guarantee future performance.

# Common Misconceptions

### The S&P 500 contains exactly the 500 largest U.S. companies.

Not necessarily.

The S&P 500 is constructed according to specific eligibility and index-maintenance rules. It is designed to represent large-cap U.S. equities rather than simply selecting the 500 largest companies by market capitalization.

---

### The Dow tracks the whole U.S. stock market.

False.

The Dow tracks 30 large U.S. companies.

It is therefore much narrower than broader market indices.

---

### Nasdaq is the same thing as the Nasdaq Composite.

Not exactly.

Nasdaq is a stock exchange and financial-market company, while the Nasdaq Composite is a market index.

---

### Every company in the S&P 500 has the same influence.

False.

The S&P 500 is float-adjusted market-cap weighted, so larger companies generally have greater influence.

---

### If the S&P 500 rises, every stock in it must rise.

False.

An index can rise even when some individual constituents decline.

---

### A stock with a 99% model rank has a 99% chance of rising.

False.

A 99% rank means the model's predicted return is higher than approximately 99% of the stocks in the comparison universe.

It is not a probability.

---

### A high-ranked stock will definitely outperform the S&P 500.

False.

The model ranking is relative to the stocks included in the model's comparison universe.

A high-ranked stock can still decline or underperform the broader market.

No.
