# Market Sentiment

## Overview

Market sentiment describes the overall attitude or expectations of investors toward a financial market, asset, company, or sector.

Sentiment can influence buying and selling behaviour and therefore affect asset prices.

Common descriptions include:

* Bullish sentiment.
* Bearish sentiment.
* Positive sentiment.
* Negative sentiment.
* Neutral sentiment.

Market sentiment can change quickly in response to:

* Economic news.
* Company announcements.
* Earnings reports.
* Interest-rate decisions.
* Political events.
* Geopolitical events.
* Market movements.
* Investor expectations.

Sentiment is one factor that can influence markets, but it does not guarantee future price movements.

---

# Bullish Sentiment

A market is described as **bullish** when investors generally expect prices to rise.

Bullish sentiment may be associated with:

* Positive economic expectations.
* Strong corporate earnings.
* Improving investor confidence.
* Expectations of lower interest rates.
* Strong market momentum.
* Positive company news.

For example:

> "Investors are bullish on technology stocks."

This means investors generally have positive expectations about the future performance of technology stocks.

---

# Bearish Sentiment

A market is described as **bearish** when investors generally expect prices to decline.

Bearish sentiment may be associated with:

* Weak economic expectations.
* Falling corporate earnings.
* Higher interest rates.
* Recession concerns.
* Negative company news.
* Increased uncertainty.

For example:

> "Investors are bearish on the market."

This means investors generally have negative expectations about future market performance.

---

# Positive and Negative Sentiment

Sentiment does not have to apply to the entire market.

It can refer to:

* A single company.
* An industry.
* A sector.
* A country.
* A particular asset class.
* The overall market.

For example:

A company could receive positive news while the overall market has negative sentiment.

Similarly, the overall market could be positive while a particular company experiences negative sentiment.

---

# Investor Psychology

Investor sentiment is closely related to investor psychology.

Investors do not always make decisions based solely on financial information.

Behaviour can be influenced by:

* Fear.
* Optimism.
* Greed.
* Uncertainty.
* Herd behaviour.
* Overconfidence.
* Loss aversion.

These behaviours can contribute to periods of strong buying or selling pressure.

---

# Herd Behaviour

Herd behaviour occurs when investors follow the actions of other investors rather than independently evaluating information.

For example:

1. A stock begins rising rapidly.
2. Investors notice the increase.
3. More investors buy the stock.
4. The additional buying pushes the price higher.
5. Other investors may buy because the price is rising.

This can contribute to momentum and potentially excessive optimism.

Herd behaviour can also occur during market declines.

---

# Fear and Greed

Fear and greed are commonly used to describe two broad emotional forces affecting investor behaviour.

### Fear

Fear can cause investors to:

* Sell investments.
* Reduce risk.
* Hold more cash.
* Avoid volatile assets.

### Greed

Greed can cause investors to:

* Take larger positions.
* Chase rapidly rising assets.
* Accept greater risk.
* Ignore warning signs.

Neither emotion provides a reliable prediction of future prices by itself.

---

# Fear and Greed Indicators

Some market indicators attempt to summarise whether investors appear unusually fearful or optimistic.

These may combine information such as:

* Market momentum.
* Volatility.
* Put and call activity.
* Market breadth.
* Safe-haven demand.
* Price strength.

Such indicators are measures of market conditions rather than guaranteed trading signals.

---

# Market Volatility and Sentiment

Volatility and sentiment are related but different concepts.

**Volatility** measures how much prices fluctuate.

**Sentiment** describes the general attitude or expectations of market participants.

A market can have:

* Positive sentiment and low volatility.
* Positive sentiment and high volatility.
* Negative sentiment and low volatility.
* Negative sentiment and high volatility.

Therefore, high volatility does not automatically mean sentiment is negative.

---

# News and Market Sentiment

News can influence investor sentiment.

Examples include:

* Earnings announcements.
* Product launches.
* Management changes.
* Regulatory decisions.
* Economic reports.
* Interest-rate announcements.
* Mergers and acquisitions.

Positive news may increase optimism.

Negative news may increase pessimism.

However, markets respond not only to whether news is positive or negative but also to how the news compares with investor expectations.

---

# Expectations Matter

Suppose a company reports:

**Revenue increased by 10%.**

This sounds positive.

However, if analysts expected revenue to increase by 20%, investors may react negatively.

Conversely, a company reporting only 5% growth could see its stock rise if investors expected growth of only 2%.

Therefore:

**Market reaction = Actual information relative to expectations**

rather than simply whether the information appears positive or negative.

---

# Earnings and Sentiment

Earnings reports can significantly affect sentiment.

Investors may examine:

* Revenue.
* Earnings per share.
* Profit margins.
* Guidance.
* Cash flow.
* Future growth expectations.

A company can report strong historical earnings while its stock falls if future guidance is weaker than expected.

---

# Analyst Sentiment

Financial analysts may publish opinions such as:

* Buy.
* Hold.
* Sell.

They may also provide:

* Price targets.
* Earnings estimates.
* Revenue forecasts.
* Growth expectations.

Analyst opinions can influence sentiment, but they are forecasts rather than guarantees.

Different analysts can reach different conclusions using similar information.

---

# Social Media Sentiment

Social media can influence investor sentiment because information and opinions can spread rapidly.

Examples include:

* Posts discussing individual stocks.
* Online investing communities.
* Financial influencers.
* News commentary.
* Investor discussions.

Social media sentiment has limitations.

Online discussions may contain:

* Incorrect information.
* Exaggeration.
* Herd behaviour.
* Promotional content.
* Emotional reactions.

Therefore, social media sentiment should not automatically be treated as reliable financial information.

---

# Sentiment Analysis

Sentiment analysis uses language-processing techniques to classify text according to its emotional or opinion-based characteristics.

For example:

> "The company reported strong earnings and raised its outlook."

A sentiment model might classify this as **positive**.

Another example:

> "The company warned that demand may weaken significantly."

This could be classified as **negative**.

Sentiment analysis can be performed using:

* Rule-based systems.
* Machine-learning models.
* Natural-language processing.
* Transformer models.
* Large language models.

---

# Sentiment Scores

A sentiment system may convert text into a numerical score.

For example:

**+1.0 → strongly positive**

**0.0 → neutral**

**-1.0 → strongly negative**

The exact interpretation depends on the model.

A sentiment score should therefore not be interpreted without understanding how the score was generated.

---

# Sentiment Does Not Equal Probability

A sentiment score of:

**0.90**

does not necessarily mean:

> "There is a 90% probability that the stock will increase."

It may simply mean that the input text was classified as strongly positive.

The meaning depends on the underlying sentiment model.

---

# Market Sentiment vs Fundamental Analysis

These concepts focus on different information.

| Market Sentiment      | Fundamental Analysis        |
| --------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Investor expectations | Company financial condition |
| Market psychology     | Revenue                     |
| News reactions        | Earnings                    |
| Optimism/pessimism    | Cash flow                   |
| Investor behaviour    | Debt                        |
| Social discussion     | Valuation                   |

They can complement each other.

For example, a company may have strong fundamentals but negative short-term sentiment.

---

# Market Sentiment vs Technical Analysis

Technical analysis focuses primarily on market data such as:

* Price.
* Volume.
* Momentum.
* Volatility.
* Trends.

Market sentiment focuses more on the attitudes and expectations of market participants.

There can be overlap because sentiment can influence price and volume.

---

# Market Sentiment vs the Financial Advisor Bot

The Financial Advisor Bot's prediction model is not simply a sentiment model.

The current model uses market-derived features such as:

* `Return_1d`
* `Return_5d`
* `Vol_20`
* `Trend_Strength`
* `Momentum_Accel`
* `Volume_Z`
* `Rolling_MaxDD`
* `Relative_Return`
* `Relative_Momentum`
* `MomentumRiskAdj`

These features describe price, volume, momentum, volatility, and relative performance.

The model then predicts future returns and ranks stocks relative to one another.

Therefore:

**Market sentiment ≠ model predicted return**

---

# Predicted Return vs Sentiment

Suppose the chatbot produces:

**Predicted Return: +0.86%**

This means the model predicts a positive return over the target prediction period.

It does not necessarily mean:

> "Investors are 86% bullish."

Similarly, a negative predicted return does not necessarily mean that all investors are bearish.

The model prediction and market sentiment measure different concepts.

---

# Example: Tesla

Suppose the chatbot receives:

> "Should I buy Tesla?"

The model might produce:

**Predicted Return: +0.86%**

**Market Rank: 96.9%**

**Signal: BUY**

This indicates that Tesla has a relatively strong predicted return within the model's comparison universe.

It does not necessarily mean:

* 96.9% of investors are bullish.
* There is a 96.9% probability Tesla will rise.
* Tesla has a 96.9% chance of making a profit.
* The stock will definitely increase.

The rank is a **relative model ranking**, not a sentiment or probability score.

# News Sentiment Example

Suppose a company announces:

> "The company expects strong revenue growth next year."

A sentiment system may classify the statement as positive.

The chatbot could potentially record:

**News Sentiment = +0.75**

However, the stock could still decline if investors expected an even stronger outlook.

This demonstrates why sentiment should be treated as one input rather than a guaranteed trading signal.

# Sentiment and Market Timing

Sentiment can change quickly.

For example:

**Morning**

Positive earnings → positive sentiment

**Afternoon**

Unexpected economic announcement → negative sentiment

Therefore, sentiment-based information can become outdated quickly.

A sentiment system should consider:

* Publication time.
* Source.
* Recency.
* Relevance.
* Whether the information has already been incorporated into prices.

Sources should be evaluated for:

* Reliability.

# Limitations of Sentiment Analysis

Sentiment analysis has several limitations.

## Sarcasm

A statement may appear positive while actually being negative.

## Context

The meaning of a sentence can depend on surrounding information.

## Ambiguity

Words can have different meanings depending on context.

## Source Bias

Some sources may intentionally promote a particular viewpoint.

## Rapidly Changing Information

Sentiment can change after new information becomes available.

## False Information

Online sources can contain inaccurate or misleading claims.

The RAG knowledge base can contain information about:

* Bullish and bearish markets.
* Investor psychology.
* Market volatility.
* News interpretation.

RAG retrieves relevant information from the knowledge base before generating an explanation.

# Best Practices

When interpreting market sentiment:

* Consider multiple information sources.
* Check when information was published.
* Distinguish opinions from facts.
* Avoid treating sentiment as a probability.
* Consider investor expectations.
* Avoid relying solely on social media.
* Consider fundamentals and market data as well.
* Remember that sentiment can change quickly.

# Common Misconceptions

### Bullish means the stock will definitely rise.

False.

Bullish describes positive expectations, not a guaranteed outcome.

---

### Bearish means a stock will definitely fall.

False.

Bearish describes negative expectations.

---

### A positive news article means the stock should increase.

Not necessarily.

The market reaction depends on expectations and other information.

---

### A sentiment score is a probability.

Not necessarily.

A sentiment score represents the output of a particular sentiment-analysis system and may not represent the probability of a price increase.

---

### A high model rank means strong investor sentiment.

False.

The Financial Advisor Bot's rank represents the relative position of the model's predicted return.

---

### RAG predicts stock prices.

No.

RAG retrieves information from the knowledge base to help generate explanations. The machine-learning model is responsible for generating stock predictions.
