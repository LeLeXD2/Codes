# Portfolio Management

## Overview

Portfolio management is the process of selecting, managing, and monitoring a collection of investments.

A portfolio can contain different types of assets, including:

* Stocks.
* Bonds.
* ETFs.
* Mutual funds.
* Cash.
* Other investments.

Portfolio management considers both **potential returns and risk**.

The objective is not simply to find the investment with the highest predicted return. It is also important to consider how investments interact with one another and how much risk the overall portfolio carries.

---

# What Is a Portfolio?

A portfolio is a collection of investments owned by an investor.

For example:

| Asset   | Allocation |
| ------- | ---------: |
| Stock A |        30% |
| Stock B |        25% |
| Stock C |        20% |
| ETF     |        15% |
| Cash    |        10% |

The allocation determines how much of the portfolio is exposed to each investment.

---

# Asset Allocation

Asset allocation describes how investments are distributed between different asset classes.

For example, a portfolio might contain:

* 60% stocks.
* 30% bonds.
* 10% cash.

Another investor may have:

* 80% stocks.
* 15% bonds.
* 5% cash.

The appropriate allocation depends on factors such as:

* Investment objectives.
* Time horizon.
* Risk tolerance.
* Financial circumstances.

There is no universally correct asset allocation.

---

# Diversification

Diversification means spreading investments across different assets rather than concentrating the entire portfolio in one investment.

For example:

**Concentrated portfolio**

* 90% Company A.
* 10% Cash.

**Diversified portfolio**

* 20% Company A.
* 20% Company B.
* 20% Company C.
* 20% ETF.
* 20% Bonds.

Diversification can reduce the effect that one investment has on the overall portfolio.

However, diversification cannot eliminate all investment risk.

---

# Why Diversification Matters

Suppose an investor has $10,000.

If the entire amount is invested in one stock and that stock falls by 30%:

**Loss = $3,000**

If the same $10,000 is spread across multiple investments, a 30% decline in one holding may have a smaller effect on the overall portfolio.

However, diversification does not guarantee that the portfolio will make money.

---

# Correlation

Correlation describes how two investments tend to move relative to each other.

A correlation close to:

**+1**

means two assets tend to move in the same direction.

A correlation close to:

**0**

means their movements have relatively little linear relationship.

A correlation close to:

**-1**

means they tend to move in opposite directions.

Assets with lower correlations can potentially provide greater diversification benefits.

However, correlations can change over time, especially during periods of market stress.

---

# Position Sizing

Position sizing determines how much capital is allocated to a particular investment.

For example:

An investor has a $20,000 portfolio.

A 5% position means:

**$20,000 × 0.05 = $1,000**

Position sizing is important because even a strong investment prediction can be wrong.

A smaller position limits the effect of an individual investment on the total portfolio.

---

# Concentration Risk

Concentration risk occurs when a large portion of a portfolio is exposed to one investment, sector, industry, or risk factor.

For example, a portfolio containing:

* 40% NVIDIA.
* 30% AMD.
* 20% Intel.
* 10% Other assets.

may appear to contain multiple stocks, but it still has substantial exposure to the semiconductor industry.

Diversification should therefore consider more than just the number of holdings.

---

# Sector Diversification

Sector diversification involves spreading investments across different economic sectors.

Examples include:

* Technology.
* Healthcare.
* Financials.
* Energy.
* Consumer goods.
* Industrials.
* Utilities.
* Communication services.

Investing in several companies within the same sector does not necessarily provide the same diversification as investing across different sectors.

---

# Geographic Diversification

Investors can also diversify across countries and regions.

For example, a portfolio may contain:

* U.S. stocks.
* European stocks.
* Asian stocks.
* Emerging-market investments.

Geographic diversification can reduce dependence on one country's economic conditions.

However, international investments can introduce additional risks such as:

* Currency risk.
* Political risk.
* Regulatory differences.
* Different market structures.

---

# Rebalancing

Rebalancing is the process of adjusting a portfolio back toward its desired allocation.

Suppose the target allocation is:

* 60% stocks.
* 40% bonds.

After stocks rise substantially, the portfolio may become:

* 70% stocks.
* 30% bonds.

An investor may rebalance by reducing the stock allocation and increasing the bond allocation.

---

# Why Rebalancing Matters

Without rebalancing, portfolio allocations can drift over time.

This can cause the portfolio to become:

* More concentrated.
* More risky.
* Different from the investor's original plan.

Rebalancing can help maintain the intended risk profile.

However, rebalancing may involve:

* Transaction costs.
* Taxes.
* Trading costs.

The frequency of rebalancing should therefore be considered carefully.

---

# Strategic vs Tactical Allocation

## Strategic Allocation

Strategic allocation establishes a long-term target.

For example:

* 70% stocks.
* 20% bonds.
* 10% cash.

The investor generally maintains these allocations over a long period.

## Tactical Allocation

Tactical allocation temporarily changes allocations based on market conditions or investment views.

For example, an investor might temporarily increase exposure to stocks because they believe market conditions are favourable.

Tactical strategies generally involve more active decision-making.

---

# Risk and Return

Portfolio management involves balancing potential return with risk.

A portfolio with a higher allocation to volatile assets may experience larger price fluctuations.

A portfolio with a lower allocation to volatile assets may experience smaller fluctuations, although this does not guarantee lower losses.

Risk should therefore be evaluated at the **portfolio level**, not only at the individual-stock level.

---

# Expected Return

Expected return represents an estimate of the return an investment or portfolio may produce.

It is important to distinguish:

**Expected return**

from:

**Guaranteed return**

An expected return is an estimate based on assumptions or a model.

It is not a promise of future performance.

---

# Portfolio Return

A portfolio's return depends on the returns of its individual holdings and their portfolio weights.

A simplified formula is:

**Portfolio Return = Σ (Weight × Asset Return)**

For example:

| Asset | Weight | Return |
| ----- | -----: | -----: |
| A     |    50% |    10% |
| B     |    30% |     5% |
| C     |    20% |    -2% |

Portfolio return:

**(0.50 × 10%) + (0.30 × 5%) + (0.20 × -2%)**

= **6.1%**

---

# Portfolio Risk

Portfolio risk depends on:

* Individual asset volatility.
* Portfolio weights.
* Correlations between assets.
* Market conditions.

This means that simply adding more assets does not automatically produce a low-risk portfolio.

The relationships between the assets also matter.

---

# Volatility and Portfolio Management

Volatility measures how much an asset's returns fluctuate.

An investor may choose to limit exposure to highly volatile investments.

For example:

**Portfolio A**

* 80% low-volatility assets.
* 20% high-volatility assets.

**Portfolio B**

* 20% low-volatility assets.
* 80% high-volatility assets.

Portfolio B will generally have greater exposure to the price movements of volatile assets.

However, volatility is only one measure of risk.

---

# Maximum Drawdown

Maximum drawdown measures the largest decline from a previous peak to a subsequent low.

For example:

Portfolio value reaches:

**$20,000**

and later falls to:

**$15,000**

Drawdown:

**($15,000 - $20,000) ÷ $20,000 = -25%**

Maximum drawdown is useful for understanding historical downside.

It does not guarantee that future losses will remain within the historical maximum.

---

# Sharpe Ratio

The Sharpe ratio is commonly used to evaluate return relative to volatility.

A simplified formula is:

**Sharpe Ratio = (Portfolio Return - Risk-Free Rate) ÷ Portfolio Volatility**

A higher Sharpe ratio generally indicates more return relative to the volatility taken.

However, the Sharpe ratio has limitations and should not be used as the only portfolio-performance measure.

---

# Sortino Ratio

The Sortino ratio is similar to the Sharpe ratio but focuses more specifically on downside volatility.

It attempts to distinguish harmful downside movements from general volatility.

This can make it useful when investors are particularly concerned about downside risk.

---

# Transaction Costs

Trading can involve costs such as:

* Brokerage fees.
* Bid-ask spreads.
* Market impact.
* Taxes in some jurisdictions.

Frequent trading can cause these costs to accumulate.

Therefore, a strategy that appears profitable before transaction costs may perform substantially worse after costs are included.

---

# Portfolio Turnover

Portfolio turnover measures how frequently investments are bought and sold.

Higher turnover generally means more trading activity.

Higher turnover can increase:

* Transaction costs.
* Tax considerations.
* Operational complexity.

A strategy should therefore be evaluated using realistic trading assumptions.

---

# Portfolio Management and the Financial Advisor Bot

The Financial Advisor Bot ranks stocks according to predicted future returns.

For example, the model may produce:

| Stock   | Predicted Return | Rank | Signal |
| ------- | ---------------: | ---: | ------ |
| Stock A |             2.1% |  99% | BUY    |
| Stock B |             1.5% |  95% | BUY    |
| Stock C |             0.4% |  60% | HOLD   |
| Stock D |            -1.0% |  10% | HOLD   |
| Stock E |            -2.0% |   2% | SELL   |

The ranking identifies stocks that the model expects to perform relatively better or worse.

However, **ranking stocks is not the same as constructing a complete portfolio**.

---

# Market Rank

The Financial Advisor Bot calculates a cross-sectional rank.

For example:

**Market Rank = 96.9%**

means the stock's predicted return is higher than approximately 96.9% of the stocks in the comparison universe.

It does **not** mean:

> There is a 96.9% probability that the stock will increase.

This distinction is important.

---

# BUY, HOLD and SELL

The chatbot uses the model's ranking to generate signals.

For example:

**Top 5% → BUY**

**Middle range → HOLD**

**Bottom 5% → SELL**

The exact thresholds depend on the implementation of the model.

The signals represent the model's relative predictions.

They should not be interpreted as personalised financial advice or guaranteed outcomes.

---

# Portfolio Construction Using Model Rankings

A possible portfolio system could use the model rankings to determine which stocks receive greater allocation.

For example:

| Rank                 | Possible Action                        |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Top-ranked stocks    | Consider for higher allocation         |
| Middle-ranked stocks | Consider holding or smaller allocation |
| Lowest-ranked stocks | Consider reducing exposure             |

However, a real portfolio system would also need to consider:

* Position limits.
* Sector exposure.
* Volatility.
* Correlation.
* Transaction costs.
* Liquidity.
* Portfolio risk.

---

# Example

Suppose the model identifies:

* AAPL: 98th percentile.
* MSFT: 96th percentile.
* NVDA: 94th percentile.
* JPM: 75th percentile.
* XOM: 40th percentile.

A simple ranking strategy might prioritise AAPL, MSFT, and NVDA.

However, allocating the entire portfolio to those three stocks could create significant technology-sector concentration.

A portfolio-management system could therefore impose position or sector limits.

---

# Position Limits

A portfolio strategy can set maximum allocations.

For example:

**Maximum position = 10%**

This means no individual stock can represent more than 10% of the portfolio.

Similarly:

**Maximum sector exposure = 30%**

would prevent one sector from dominating the portfolio.

Position limits can reduce concentration risk.

---

# Risk-Adjusted Ranking

A model's predicted return can also be considered alongside risk.

For example, two stocks might have:

| Stock | Predicted Return | Volatility |
| ----- | ---------------: | ---------: |
| A     |             2.0% |       High |
| B     |             1.8% |        Low |

Although Stock A has a higher predicted return, Stock B may provide a different risk-return trade-off.

A risk-adjusted portfolio system could therefore consider both expected return and risk.

---

# The Difference Between a Signal and a Portfolio

A **signal** answers:

> "Which stocks does the model currently favour?"

A **portfolio** answers:

> "How should capital be distributed among investments?"

These are different problems.

The Financial Advisor Bot's current model primarily solves the first problem.

Portfolio construction is a separate layer that can be added on top of the model.

---

# Portfolio Management Limitations

A machine-learning ranking model cannot determine an appropriate portfolio for every investor without additional information.

For example, different investors may have different:

* Risk tolerance.
* Time horizons.
* Financial goals.
* Existing investments.
* Liquidity requirements.

Therefore, the chatbot should avoid claiming that one portfolio allocation is appropriate for everyone.

---

# Best Practices

When managing a portfolio:

* Diversify across investments and risk factors.
* Consider position sizes.
* Monitor sector concentration.
* Consider correlations between assets.
* Rebalance when appropriate.
* Account for transaction costs.
* Consider the investment time horizon.
* Evaluate risk as well as expected return.
* Avoid relying on a single model or indicator.
* Review whether the portfolio still matches the investor's objectives.

# Common Misconceptions

### The highest-ranked stock should receive all the money.

Not necessarily.

The highest-ranked stock may also have substantial volatility or concentration risk.

---

### More stocks always mean more diversification.

Not necessarily.

Owning many companies from the same sector may still create significant concentration.

---

### Rebalancing guarantees better returns.

False.

Rebalancing is primarily a portfolio-management technique and does not guarantee higher returns.

---

### A diversified portfolio cannot lose money.

False.

Diversification can reduce certain types of risk but cannot eliminate market risk.

---

### A BUY signal means I should buy the stock.

Not automatically.

A BUY signal represents the model's prediction. An investor should also consider their own circumstances, portfolio, risk tolerance, and investment objectives.

# Frequently Asked Questions

### What is portfolio management?

Portfolio management is the process of selecting, allocating, monitoring, and adjusting investments within a portfolio.

---

### What is asset allocation?

Asset allocation describes how a portfolio's capital is distributed across different asset classes.

---

---

---

Rebalancing adjusts a portfolio back toward its intended allocation after market movements cause the weights to change.

---

Concentration risk occurs when too much of a portfolio is exposed to one investment, sector, country, or risk factor.

---

### Does the Financial Advisor Bot construct a complete portfolio?

The current model primarily ranks stocks and generates BUY/HOLD/SELL signals. A full portfolio-construction system would require additional rules for allocation, diversification, position limits, and risk management.

---

Not necessarily.

A higher rank indicates stronger relative model predictions, but portfolio allocation should also consider risk and diversification.
